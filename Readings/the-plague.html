Literary Reflection

Reflections on Duty Without Reason

A reflection on Albert Camus' The Plague

Anirudh Arora
Research Assistant · MPI-M
NovelAlbert CamusAbsurdism

The Plague is often read as an allegory, a city under siege standing together against a common enemy. What stayed with me was a narrower question that the book never asks aloud: what does a person owe to a situation that will not answer to his effort?

The city acts as one body. The gates close, and everyone is sealed into the same sentence. But the suffering does not arrive evenly, and neither do the responses. Having read The Stranger and The Myth of Sisyphus first, I kept seeing the same problem put to different people. Each has to decide what to do in a world that offers no reason for what is happening to them, and no two decide the same way. The plague is shared. The absurd, it turns out, is still faced alone, even in a crowd.

The Doctor Who Would Not Be a Hero

At first I admired Rieux without reservation. He could have stopped and did not. But the admiration became complicated when I noticed it was mine and not his. Rieux does not experience what he does as courage, and he is careful to say so. Heroism needs a reason, something the act is for, and Rieux has none to offer. He does not believe the work will win, and he does not believe anything beyond it will redeem it. What he has is a refusal to let the absence of a reason become a reason to stop. That is different from hope, and closer to what Camus means in The Myth of Sisyphus by living without appeal.

I have noticed a smaller version of this in my own days. Continuing is rarely a decision. It is what remains after the question of whether to continue has quietly stopped being asked, and much of what I would call perseverance is a habit that outlived its need for justification.

Personal reflection

The Stranger Who Stayed

Rambert holds the most defensible position in the book, which is why it is the most instructive. He is a journalist from Paris who happens to be in Oran when the gates close, and he insists, reasonably, that none of this is his. His lover is elsewhere. He does not belong to the town. Rieux does not argue with him, and tells him there is no shame in choosing happiness.

At first I sympathized with him completely. Eventually that shifted, though not because he does anything wrong. On the night he could finally leave, he stays. He gives no principled reason, only that it might be shameful to be happy alone. Nothing has been refuted. It reads less like a conviction than like a man discovering that a belonging he had disowned was already formed without his consent. Meursault stays outside to the end and is condemned for it. Rambert begins outside and is drawn in without ever being persuaded. After The Myth of Sisyphus, where the absurd man's happiness is entirely his own, it made me wonder what that happiness means if it is held apart from everyone else.

The Man Who Was Almost Happy

Cottard is the character I did not know what to do with. He profits from the plague, working the black market while everyone around him is afraid, and he is at ease in a way no one else in the city is. At first I read him as the obvious contrast to Rieux, the man who feeds on what the doctor endures. Eventually that reading became hard to hold. Cottard was living like someone in hiding long before the gates closed, afraid of an arrest the book never fully explains. The plague is the first time the rest of the city shares his condition. Everyone is confined, everyone is afraid, everyone is waiting for a knock at the door. For a short while he is not the exception.

That is what made him uncomfortable to think about. I felt no sympathy for what he does, but I understood what the plague gave him, which was company. When it lifts, he loses it. The city celebrates and he comes apart, firing on the street from his window and taken away while the crowd is still dancing. It felt like the most honest picture of the ending in the whole book. The plague ends for everyone, and for some people that is the loss.

The Ending

The ending unsettled me more than anything before it. The plague leaves, but not because anyone defeated it. It recedes on its own, and the effort of the whole city turns out not to have been what decided the outcome. The last pages add that the bacillus never dies, only waits. Rieux, who gave everything, is left with almost nothing. Tarrou is gone. His wife is gone. The celebration around him belongs to other people. Rambert, who nearly left, is reunited with his wife on the platform. I could not tell whether that was mercy or a reminder that the book distributes nothing in proportion to what was given.

What I felt was sadness for Rieux, which surprised me, because he so clearly asks for none. The devastation was not that the effort failed. It was that effort and outcome turned out to be separate things. If an act can only be justified by what it achieves, then Rieux's work was wasted. If it can be justified in any other way, the book does not say how, and he continues anyway.

The Line That Stayed

The moment I keep returning to is small. Tarrou speaks of wanting to be a saint without God, which is already an ambitious thing to want. Rieux declines even that. He feels more fellowship with the defeated than with saints, and says that what interests him is being a man.

The modesty of the line is what makes it hard to hold. Sainthood and heroism come with something attached, a meaning or a reward. Being a man, as Rieux uses the phrase, comes with nothing. It is simply what one does when the alternative is to stop.

What It Left Me With

The Plague felt to me like a conclusion. Meursault showed the absurd endured alone. Sisyphus made the argument. This book shows what it looks like among other people, when it becomes a matter of duty and there is no philosophy left to hide behind.

If the work does not change the ending, what makes it worth doing? Rieux never asks. I could not tell whether that is wisdom or exhaustion, and I am no longer sure the two are different.
