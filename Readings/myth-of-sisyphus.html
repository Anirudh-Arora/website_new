Literary Reflection

Reflections on Routine, Revolt, and Living Without Appeal

A reflection on Albert Camus' The Myth of Sisyphus

Anirudh Arora
Research Assistant · MPI-M
PhilosophyAlbert CamusAbsurdism

The Myth of Sisyphus is often approached as a philosophical argument against suicide, a response to the question of whether life, stripped of meaning, is worth living. What stayed with me was something less dramatic and more difficult to sit with. The argument felt true.

Not true in the way a clever observation feels true, where you nod and move on. True in the way that makes you set the book down and look at your own life for a moment. Camus is not describing an extreme condition. He is describing the texture of ordinary consciousness: the feeling, arriving without warning, that the habits and routines and purposes you have built around yourself cannot ultimately be justified. That the universe has no interest in confirming any of it. Most people, he suggests, sense this and immediately look away. The discomfort of the essay is that it does not let you.

The Argument and What It Actually Claims

Camus refuses both available exits. Suicide is not an answer because it surrenders to the absurd rather than living against it. Religious belief is not an answer because it resolves the tension by pretending it is not there, by leaping toward a certainty the evidence does not support. What he calls philosophical suicide is in some ways harder to argue with than physical suicide, because it is so much more comfortable. The leap toward God, or toward ideology, or toward any system that promises coherence, is a recognizable human movement. Camus simply refuses to make it. He wants to find out, as he puts it, whether it is possible to live without appeal.

What was unexpected was that this refusal did not read as cold or nihilistic. It read as a kind of clarity. The absurd man, for Camus, is not despairing. He is honest.

Personal reflection

The Routine and What It Actually Is

The conclusion, one must imagine Sisyphus happy, landed for me not as a philosophical proposition but as something I recognized from my own life. My days lately follow a pattern I have largely constructed myself. The same rhythms, the same work, the same movement through the same hours. And I am, on reflection, at peace with this. Not because I have found meaning in it in some grand sense, but because I have stopped requiring grand sense from it. Occasionally I break the routine, do something different, and return to it with something that feels like renewed willingness. The boulder rolls back down. I walk after it.

I did not expect a book about Greek mythology and the philosophy of the absurd to describe my commute so precisely.

What the Argument Does Not Resolve

Camus is careful about what he is claiming. The preceding merely defines a way of thinking, he writes near the end. But the point is to live. That sentence is doing more work than it appears to. It acknowledges that the argument is not the same as the life. Knowing the absurd is the structure of existence does not automatically produce the revolt he describes, the conscious, clear-eyed persistence in the face of it. Awareness and practice are not the same thing, and Camus knows this.

One line stayed with me apart from the main argument: a man is more a man through the things he keeps to himself than through those he says. It appears almost in passing. But it sits strangely beside an essay that is so committed to lucidity, to saying the thing plainly. I kept thinking about what Camus was not saying. What the absurd, fully confronted, actually feels like from the inside, on a Tuesday morning, when the boulder is at the bottom again.

What It Left Me With

The Myth of Sisyphus does not comfort you exactly. It offers something stranger than comfort, which is the suggestion that the absence of comfort is survivable, and that living in full awareness of that absence is not resignation but its opposite.

Would Sisyphus be happy if he knew someone was watching? I am not sure the question matters to Camus. The happiness, if it exists, is not performed. It is just what persisting, eyes open, eventually looks like from the inside.
